# EGIZ — THE SAME GROUND
### Cinematic art direction for the landing experience

> AI-assisted creative direction; human review pending. Everything generated from this guide is
> **illustrative footage**. It shows no real mission, crew, partner or result, and the page labels it so.

---

## 1. The idea in one line

**Every alien ground has a twin on Earth. The film finds it.**

EGIZ measures how closely a place on Earth reproduces a place on the Moon or Mars, and says plainly
what no place on Earth can reproduce. The cinema says the same thing in pictures. Its signature device is
the **twin cut**: an alien shot and an Earth shot made with *identical* camera geometry (the same
height, lens, horizon line, sun azimuth and direction of travel), cut or dissolved so that for a moment
the viewer cannot tell which world they are on. Then one detail gives it away: the sky, a footprint, dust
that does not settle.

No other space website can use that device honestly, because the device *is* the product.

## 2. The three laws

1. **Grazing light.** The Sun is always low: 2–12° above the horizon. Low light is how
   geologists read relief, and relief (slope, roughness, rock fields) is what EGIZ measures. Every shot
   is lit to make terrain *legible*, never flat.
2. **Measured scale.** Every frame carries a scale cue: a figure, a rover, a boot or a tread
   mark, small and never posed. The landscapes are monumental because something human is in them, not
   because the lens is wide.
3. **Nothing glows that would not glow.** No holograms, no neon, no HUD overlays, no lens-flare
   decoration, no floating particles that are not dust. Information on the page is typography; the footage
   is the world.

## 3. Colour science

| World | Sky | Lit ground | Shadow | Accent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Moon** | true black, stars only when exposure allows | warm neutral grey, faint beige (≈ #8f8a82) | near-black with a cool earthshine lift (≈ #0b0e14) | a thin blue Earth crescent |
| **Mars** | butterscotch by day (≈ #c99a6b), pale blue around a low Sun (≈ #a9bccf) | oxidised ochre and rust (≈ #9a5a32) | cool brown-violet (≈ #2a1d1c), never black | dust backlit into gold |
| **Earth stand-ins** | clean natural blue to pale gold at the horizon | salt white, basalt black, desert ochre | neutral, slightly cool | one human-coloured object (orange suit, tool) |
| **Orbit** | black | thin atmosphere shell: orange → cream → blue → violet | | the Moon, small and sharp |

- Grade: filmic ACES-like roll-off, soft highlight shoulder, **shadows never crushed to 0**. Keep
  detail in crater floors.
- Saturation is earned: Moon at ~80% (it really is almost colourless), Mars at ~105%, Earth at 95%.
- Split tone: warm highlights, cool shadows, on every world, so the footage cuts together.
- Fine organic grain at 35 mm 400 ISO character. **No** digital noise, sharpening halos or chromatic
  aberration.

## 4. Camera language

- **Heavy and deliberate.** Dolly, crane, slow orbital drift, locked-off time-lapse. No handheld shake,
  whip pans, snap zooms or drone "reveal spins".
- **Speeds:** ground shots move at walking pace or slower; aerials glide at ≤ 30 m/s apparent; orbit
  drifts imperceptibly.
- **Lenses:** 24 mm for monumental ground shots, 35–50 mm for twins and people, 85–135 mm compressed
  telephoto for horizons, the limb and dust columns, 100 mm macro for regolith.
- **Composition:** horizon on the upper third (ground dominates; that is where the data lives) or at the
  very top for macro. Subjects sit off-centre on the side *opposite* the page's text column (see §7).
- **Duration:** 10 s per clip. Each clip has a clear beginning frame and end frame (Flow keyframes) so
  the website can dissolve into and out of it.

## 5. Materials and atmosphere

- **Regolith:** fine, cohesive, slightly clumpy; boot prints keep razor-sharp edges; no dust plume
  hangs in the air on the Moon (it falls ballistically).
- **Rock:** angular breccia on the Moon; layered sedimentary benches, dark basaltic sand and ventifacts
  on Mars; salt polygons, alluvial fans and varnished ranges on Earth.
- **Air:** none on the Moon (perfectly sharp to the horizon). Thin but dusty on Mars (strong haze with
  distance, a blue-white aureole around the Sun at dusk). Earth haze is blue and gentle.

## 6. Rhythm of the film

One continuous 64-second shot in eight 8-second Veo 3.1 clips, chained frame to frame (see
[SCENES.md](SCENES.md)):

`ARRIVAL (vast) → THE TWIN (recognition) → RETURN (silence) → PASSAGE (speed) → JEZERO (beauty) →
THE POINTING (a human hand) → THE ANSWER (the match) → HOME (orbit)`

## 7. How footage meets the page

- **Footage → instrument hand-off.** Interactive chapters open on film, then dissolve into the
  real-time measured 3D world, which has matching framing. The page says when you are looking at the
  live instrument. Footage never sits over an interaction.
- **Film-only moments.** Mars and the finale play as pure cinema with minimal type.
- **Text column.** On desktop, copy lives in a column (left or right per chapter). Keep the subject of
  each shot on the other side; leave the copy side darker and calmer (darker ground, sky or shadow).
- **Phones.** Copy sits in the lower half. Keep the upper 45% of every frame strong on its own
  (horizon, subject, Sun). The page crops the 16:9 frame to the centre on portrait screens, so keep the
  subject within the middle 56% of the width.

## 8. What we never show

- Real agency logos, flags, mission patches, named spacecraft or an identifiable real rover.
- Anything implying EGIZ flew, landed or was used on a mission.
- Fake telemetry, fake UI, fake text on screens (generated text is gibberish anyway).
- Recognisable real people.
